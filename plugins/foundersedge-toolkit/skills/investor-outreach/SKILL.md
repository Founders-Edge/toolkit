# Investor Outreach — Target List & Warm Paths

Builds a qualified, tiered investor target list, maps the strongest access path to each name, and
drafts the actual messages — so the founder shows up in front of *fitting* investors carrying
someone's trust, not in a pile of 400 cold decks. Tuned for **US angel/pre-seed/seed** raises,
with Series A notes.

> **Founders:** the data is unambiguous on two points. (1) **Fit beats volume** — DocSend's
> research found no correlation between the number of investors contacted and the amount raised;
> 50 qualified targets beat 500 loose names. (2) **The channel matters less than the signal** —
> warm intros convert far better than cold email, but researched, partner-level, thesis-matched
> cold email works, and a weak intro from someone the investor barely knows is worth roughly
> nothing. This skill optimizes both: a tight list, reached through the strongest path you have.

---

## Workflow

```
1. IDENTIFY   → Company, stage, round, traction, geography, timeline, who the founder knows
2. PROFILE    → Pin the stage; fit filters (stage, check size, sector/thesis, geo) + signal inventory
3. LIST       → Build the long list, qualify hard, pick the partner, screen conflicts
4. TIER & MAP → Tier A/B/C; assign every investor an access path (relationship/warm/inbound/cold)
5. DRAFT      → Intro requests + forwardable emails for warm paths; cold emails for the rest
6. SEQUENCE   → Calendar packing: compressed window, parallel waves, honest momentum signals
7. MEETINGS   → When an investor responds: scheduling, who attends, conversation not slide-show
8. TRACK      → Pipeline spreadsheet (Google Sheets/Excel) with an "intro owner" per warm path
```

## Step 1: Identify

Ask if not given (one short batch — don't re-ask what's already known):
- Company, what it does and for whom, sector, stage (angel / pre-seed / seed / Series A), round
  size and instrument
- Traction: revenue/users/growth, signed customers, anything already committed to the round
- Geography (team + where they can take meetings)
- Timeline: raising now, or building relationships ahead of a raise?
- **Network inventory** — this drives everything downstream: founders they know who've raised
  (and from whom), any investors they've met, angels in their orbit, lawyers/accountants/
  accelerator staff, and anyone at a target fund's portfolio companies

---

## Step 2: Fit profile

**Pin the stage first — then target only firms that write checks in that range.** The single
most common list-building error is pitching investors one stage up (or down). Calibrate against
current market norms:

| Stage | Typical shape (US, 2025–26 norms) | Who to target |
|-------|-----------------------------------|---------------|
| **Angel** | $10K–$100K individual checks, often pre-product; SAFEs | Individual angels, operator angels, angel groups, syndicate leads |
| **Pre-seed** | Rounds mostly < $1M (many < $250K); ~90% on SAFEs; caps ~$7.5–15M | Dedicated pre-seed funds, angels, rolling funds, accelerators |
| **Seed** | Median ~$4M; SAFEs dominate below ~$4M, priced above; institutional leads | Seed funds, multi-stage firms' seed programs, large syndicates |
| **Series A** | Priced equity round; typically ~20 months after seed, with real revenue/usage traction | Lead-capable Series A firms, partner-level targeting only |

Three stage-specific rules:
- **Check the fund's definition, not the label.** Many funds say "pre-seed" but their recent
  deals all had revenue — for this founder they're a seed fund. The last ~10 checks are the truth.
- **Angels and institutions are different motions.** Angels decide alone and fast (days), close
  on signed SAFEs one at a time, and are the practice circuit + signal engine. Funds decide in
  partnerships (weeks) and anchor rounds. Pre-seed raises usually run angels-first; a Series A is
  a funds-only process.
- **Mind the bridge question.** Nearly half of "seed" activity now is bridge/extension rounds —
  if this raise is a bridge, the target list is primarily existing investors plus funds known to
  do bridges, and the pitch is different ("what's changed since the last round").

Then define the filters every target must pass — **four qualifiers** (stage, check size,
sector/thesis, geography) plus:
- **Judge funds by their last ~10 actual checks, not their website copy.** A fund that says
  "pre-seed" but whose recent deals all had revenue or a priced round is not a pre-seed fund for
  this founder. Recent deals are the thesis.
- **Fund cycle**: a firm late in deploying its current fund, or that hasn't announced a new one
  in years, is a low-probability target.
- **Honest signal inventory**: what does this founder have that makes an investor lean in —
  traction, team background, notable customers, committed checks? If the honest answer is "not
  much yet," say so kindly: outreach mechanics can't fix a signal problem, and the best next step
  may be a committed angel or a customer before a VC push (see Step 4, "engineer the signal").

## Step 3: Build the list

**Sources** (use web search to verify anything uncertain; prefer recent information):
- **OpenVC** (free, 16,000+ investors, filterable by the four qualifiers) and **NFX Signal**
  (free, maps warm paths through the founder's own network) — the standard free stack
- **Crunchbase** for recent deals by stage/sector, and the portfolio pages of comparable
  (non-competing) companies — whoever funded them funds things like this
- **Ask founder friends point-blank who wrote their first check** — the single highest-yield
  list-building question
- Published open-inbox lists of VCs who take cold pitches, and rolling-fund GPs who solicit
  publicly (legally allowed to — no intro needed)

**Rules:**
- **Target partners, not firms.** Pick the named partner whose thesis and past deals fit (plus a
  backup partner). An intro to "the fund" goes nowhere.
- **Screen out firms with competing portfolio companies** — pitching them leaks information.
- **Never invent.** Don't fabricate a fund's thesis, portfolio, check size, or partner names. If
  it can't be verified, mark it `[VERIFY]` with a note on how to check (e.g., "confirm on
  Crunchbase: deals since 2024").
- **Size:** a core target list of **20–50 firms**, drawn from a longer researched pool (100–200
  names is normal). Survey data: successful pre-seed/seed raisers contacted 58–71 investors on
  average — plan the pipeline for that, but build it from qualified names, not spray.

## Step 4: Tier and map access paths

**Tier the list:**
- **Tier A** — dream leads: best thesis fit, lead-sized checks, the founder would take their money
  over anyone's
- **Tier B** — strong fit, credible alternatives
- **Tier C** — plausible fit + angels: real targets *and* the practice circuit

**Assign every investor an access path** — the strongest available, in this order:
1. **Existing relationship** — the founder already knows them; just email
2. **Warm intro** — rank candidate introducers honestly (see below)
3. **Inbound/event** — the investor's office hours, open form, a conference, their content
4. **Cold** — legitimate when researched and partner-matched (Step 5 template)

**Introducer ranking** (who the intro comes from matters more than that it exists):
1. An investor who **just invested in this round** — the strongest possible intro
2. A **portfolio founder** of the target fund — ideally one whose company is doing well
3. A founder or operator the investor **knows well and trusts**
4. Lawyers, accountants, accelerator staff — real but weaker

A warm intro from someone the investor barely knows, or an intro with no genuine vouch behind
it, is worth approximately zero — and can read as a negative signal. Fewer, stronger introducers
beat many weak ones.

**The portfolio-founder route** (the best-documented conversion play): for each Tier A fund, find
2–3 portfolio founders in an adjacent (non-competing) space. Reach out for a short call about
their experience taking that fund's money — advice, not an ask. Founders who've raised are
generous with this, and when the intro eventually comes *from the fund's own founder*, the
investor treats the deal as already half-in. Do not fake interest: the conversation must be real,
and the intro ask comes only after genuine rapport.

**Engineer the signal — start warm, end cold.** Close the easiest money first (angels, friendly
small checks, the founder's existing network). "We've secured a third of the round" transforms
every subsequent email — one credible angel or known-logo pilot can flip reply rates overnight.
Go cold only to fill the gaps, carrying the warm signal with you.

## Step 5: Draft the messages

Draft real, ready-to-send text — short, specific, in the founder's voice. Three templates:

### 5a. The ask-to-the-introducer (double opt-in)

Never hand an introducer homework ("can you intro me to some investors?"). Ask permission, name
the target, and attach the forwardable email so their total effort is one click:

```
Subject: Intro to [Partner] @ [Fund]?

Hi [Introducer] — I'm raising our [pre-seed/seed] and [Partner] at [Fund] looks like a strong
fit: [one specific reason — their recent investment in X / their writing on Y].

Would you be comfortable forwarding the note below? If it's not a fit or you'd rather not, no
problem at all — and happy to share anything that makes this easier.

[forwardable email below]
```

The double opt-in matters: the introducer checks with the investor before connecting, so nobody
gets an unwanted intro. Let the introducer run that step — don't pressure for an immediate
connection.

### 5b. The forwardable email

Written **as if addressed to the investor**, so the introducer literally hits Forward and adds a
one-line vouch. Fresh thread, descriptive subject, self-contained, skimmable in 30 seconds:

```
Subject: Intro Request: [Founder Name] <> [Partner Name]

[Introducer], thanks for passing this along to [Partner].

[Partner] — I'm [Name], founder of [Company]. We [one sentence: what you do and for whom].

Quick context:
- [Traction point 1 — a real number]
- [Traction point 2 — a real number or named customer]
- [Why this specific investor — their thesis, a relevant portfolio company, their writing]

We're raising [$X pre-seed/seed] and I'd love 30 minutes to show you what we're building —
deck here: [link]. I'm free [two specific windows] or anytime here: [calendar link].
```

**Forwardable-email etiquette:**
- **Fresh thread, never buried in a reply chain** — forcing copy-paste work kills intros
- When the intro lands: **reply fast, thank the introducer, move them to BCC** in the first reply
- **No reply after ~10 days → one polite nudge**, then let it go
- **Close the loop with the introducer** either way — it's how you earn the next intro

### 5c. The cold email (when no warm path exists)

Cold works when it's an obvious bullseye for this specific partner — and fails as spray:

```
Subject: [<60 characters — what the company is, not "investment opportunity"]

Hi [Partner] — I'm [Name], founder/CEO of [Company]. We [one sentence: what you do, for whom].

- [Strongest proof point — traction, growth, team, named customer]
- [Second proof point]
- [Why you: their specific investment or thesis — one line, no flattery]

Raising [$X seed]. Deck: [link — include it; don't make them ask]. Worth 20 minutes?
```

**Cold rules (from investors' own stated preferences):**
- **From the founder/CEO only** — never an agency, adviser, or "head of fundraising"
- Under ~1,000 characters; short subject lines get opened, clever ones don't
- Lead with the strongest *signal*, not forced personalization ("I see you invest in SaaS and
  we're a SaaS company" reads as filler)
- One or two follow-ups, 3–7 days apart; deck opened + no reply after follow-up = a pass
- **No mail-merge, no AI-generated blast, no tracking pixels.** Investors' inboxes are flooded
  with templated AI outreach and they spot it in seconds; it burns the founder's name. Budget
  10–15 minutes per email. If that feels too slow, the list is too long.

## Step 6: Sequence the outreach — pack the calendar, earn the FOMO

Urgency in a raise is manufactured with a **calendar**, not with claims. Investors move when
other investors are moving; the founder's job is to make that literally true by compressing the
process so everyone is deciding in the same window.

**Calendar packing:**
- **Parallel, never serial.** Breadth-first across the list, weighted by fit. Waiting on one
  investor's answer before contacting the next is the classic first-timer error.
- **Launch each wave on the same day** so first meetings land inside one 2–3 week window, and
  **stack meeting days next to each other** — 3–5 pitches/day is a sustainable sprint ceiling.
  One call a week builds no momentum. If an investor offers a date outside the window: "I'm doing
  most first meetings over the next week or two."
- **Name a date for each phase** and hold it: first meetings done by X, partner meetings by Y,
  decisions by Z. Stating process facts ("we're taking first meetings the week of the 14th")
  creates real synchronized pressure without claiming anything false. Gate the data room to one
  release date so diligence starts simultaneously.
- **Practice first.** Open with ~5 Tier C investors/angels to pressure-test the pitch before any
  Tier A meeting — but don't save all of Tier A for last either; mix 1–2 top choices into each
  wave after the pitch is sharp so they don't arrive asynchronously to a stale process.
- **Align the partner meetings.** VC partnerships decide at their Monday partner meetings — aim
  to land Tier A partner meetings in the same 1–2 weeks so term sheets arrive together. A proven
  shape: all first meetings in ~14 days, partner meetings clustered on two Mondays.
- **Don't out-compress the partnerships.** Demanding term sheets days after a first meeting
  loses firms that need partnership approval. Decision cycles run roughly: first pitch → answer
  in ~48h; partner meeting → diligence ≤ ~10 days → term sheet. ~3 weeks is a fast full cycle.
- **Fundraising mode is binary.** The founder is either raising full-time — sprint, 4–12 weeks,
  everything else deprioritized — or not raising at all. Avoid launching in August or
  mid-November through December.
- **Not raising yet?** Start relationships 6–12 months out ("lines, not dots") — send the
  investor-update skill's updates to a shortlist of target investors before the raise. The
  one-week blitz raises you read about are built on years of prior touchpoints.

**Creating FOMO honestly — facts, not adjectives:**
- **The first term sheet is the lever.** "Once you have one term sheet, everyone is on the
  clock." Everything in the sequence aims at getting the first credible offer; when it lands,
  tell the others the process fact and a real deadline, and accelerate.
- **Share concrete, verifiable process state** — "seven firms are in the data room", "we're in
  week two with second meetings underway" — and let investors conclude the urgency themselves.
  Vague hype ("lots of interest", "moving fast") is the tell investors roll their eyes at.
- **Constrain supply.** Anchor the announced round at the low end of the honest range — a round
  that is visibly 50–80% subscribed creates real scarcity, and oversubscribing beats falling
  short of an inflated target. Closing committed angels on signed SAFEs first makes the round
  concretely "filling."
- **Keep term-sheet holders anonymous** while negotiating — name the fact, not the firm.
- **Never fake it.** No invented term sheets, no "closing Friday" with nothing behind it, no
  predicted offers. Investors talk to each other, bluffs get called, and a stated deadline that
  slips announces that all the claimed leverage was fiction. Fake scarcity collapses the moment
  diligence starts — and in a small industry it follows the founder to the next raise.
- **Read passes correctly.** Treat anything short of a definite, unconditional offer as a no.
  But a good pass is pipeline: investors who pass often intro founders to better-fitting funds —
  ask. And when the sprint stalls (passes piling up, silence from most funds), that's normal at
  ~90% pass rates: bucket the silent ones, keep the waves moving, don't vent at investors.

## Step 7: When an investor responds — the first meeting

The outreach worked; don't fumble the conversion. All of this is investor-stated preference:

**Scheduling:**
- **Reply within 24 hours** — response speed is read as an operator-competence signal.
- **Offer 2–3 specific times**, with a scheduling link only as a convenience afterward ("or grab
  my calendar if easier"). A bare scheduling link to someone you're asking for money reads as a
  status move.
- **Ask for 30 minutes** and plan for 20 of substance. Short asks get accepted. First meetings
  are Zoom by default now — good audio/video, slides small, faces big.

**Who attends:**
- **The fundraising founder (normally the CEO) — alone by default.** One founder runs the entire
  raise; the rest of the team keeps the company growing (the best momentum signal there is).
- **A co-founder may come if they'll genuinely speak** — a silent attendee makes a worse
  impression than an empty chair (investors' rule of thumb: anyone in the room talks ≥25% of the
  time). Bring co-founders deliberately to later/partner meetings.
- **Never the whole team, and never an advisor, lawyer, or "fundraising consultant" fronting the
  meeting** — early-stage investors are underwriting the founder, and an intermediary reads as a
  red flag.

**Running the meeting — a conversation, not a slide show:**
- **Have the deck ready; don't open it unless asked.** Walking slides blind is the marked
  "noob" move — you learn nothing about how the story lands. Tell the story in conversation, pull
  up a specific slide only when it helps. (Formal *partner meetings* are the exception: there,
  slides are expected — bring an appendix for the hard questions.)
- **Don't skip the small talk** — investors say it tells them as much as the pitch — then lead
  with the single most exciting thing, not a warm-up. Don't bury the lede.
- **Talk at most a third of the time on your overview** and leave the rest interactive;
  interruptions are engagement. Spending 42 of 45 minutes on the deck is the classic fail.
- **Interview them too.** Fund size and where they are in deploying it, typical check, whether
  they lead, follow-on reserves, process and timeline, who the decision-maker is, portfolio
  conflicts. This is qualifying data for the tracker — and reads as competence.
- **Never ask for an NDA** (instant credibility killer), and don't lead with valuation — "don't
  optimize for valuation" is the canon.
- **Never leave without next steps.** Verbatim rule: "Never leave a meeting with an investor
  without asking what happens next."

**Follow-up:**
- **Same day, or within 24 hours**: reiterate what excited them, attach everything promised
  (deck, data), propose the concrete next step with a date. Don't retell the pitch.
- **Decode soft passes.** "Send me more info" / "keep me posted" with no scheduled next action on
  their side usually means a pass with optionality. Treat it as a no, add them to the monthly
  update list, and keep moving.

## Step 8: Track it — build the pipeline sheet

Build the founder a tracking spreadsheet in **Google Sheets or Excel** (preferred over
Notion/Airtable — investors' numbers live in spreadsheets, and the founder's will too). Create
the actual file when tools allow; otherwise give the founder this spec to build from. Updated
daily, it is the raise's single source of truth.

**Structure — one master list, everything else links to it:**

1. **Pipeline** (master tab) — **one row per investor** (partner + firm), holding the full
   journey of that investor. Pipeline is the **only tab anyone types on**; every field lives
   here exactly once. Columns grouped left→right in workflow order:
   - *Identity:* Partner · Firm · Stage focus · Check size
   - *Research (Step 2–3):* Thesis/sector fit · Recent-checks evidence · Fund cycle ·
     Conflict? · Verified?
   - *Targeting (Step 4):* Tier (A/B/C) · **Access path** (Existing / Warm intro / Inbound /
     Cold) · Wave
   - *Intro tracking (for warm-intro rows):* Intro owner · Introducer strength (1–4, per Step
     4's ranking) · Date asked · Forwardable sent? · Intro status (Asked / Opt-in / Forwarded /
     Connected / Declined / No reply)
   - *Execution (Steps 5–7):* Status · # Meetings · Last touch · **Nudge due** (computed: last
     touch + 10 days on warm-intro rows) · Next action · Next-action date
   - *Outcome:* $ Committed · Notes
2. **Warm Intros** — a **computed view, never a second data-entry table**: it automatically
   lists every Pipeline row whose access path is "Warm intro," pulling Partner, Firm, Tier,
   Intro owner, strength, dates, and intro status from Pipeline. A hand-maintained copy silently
   goes stale the moment a new warm path is added — all editing happens on Pipeline, and this
   tab carries a banner saying so. Portable implementation: a slim helper column on Pipeline
   numbers the warm-intro rows in order (IF + running COUNTIF); view row *k* pulls each field
   with INDEX/MATCH on that number, IFERROR-wrapped to blank. (Excel 365 and Google Sheets can
   do it with one FILTER formula instead, at the cost of portability to other engines.)
3. **Dashboard** — inputs (round target $, start date, founder's stage) plus computed funnel —
   every number computed from Pipeline, none hand-entered. Define the metrics exactly:
   - *Targets* = non-blank Pipeline rows · *Contacted* = rows with any status beyond
     "Researched" · *Meetings* = SUM of # Meetings (the 30–46 benchmark is total meetings) ·
     *In diligence+* = Diligence/Term sheet/Committed · *Term sheets* = Term sheet + Committed ·
     *$ Committed* = SUMIF on status "Committed" only (a number on a term-sheet row isn't money)
     · *% of round* · *Weeks elapsed* = (today − start)/7 · *Warm paths / intros in flight* =
     counts of warm-intro access rows and non-blank intro statuses.
   - The **stage input selects which end of each benchmark band applies** (seed: 58 contacted /
     30 meetings / ~12.5 weeks; pre-seed: 71 / 46 / ~20).
   - Below the funnel, a progress-vs-benchmark table using the figures from "What to expect,"
     each row giving a plain-language read (e.g., past ~7 weeks: "failed raises typically quit
     here — persist"; 100+ contacted with no term sheet: "the blocker is story/traction, not
     outreach volume").
4. **Benchmarks** — the benchmark constants with their sources, referenced by the Dashboard
   formulas so no magic numbers hide in cells.

**Linkage rules (the part that makes it a system, not four lists):**
- **One write surface.** Investor identity and all per-investor fields live once, on Pipeline;
  Warm Intros and the Dashboard are pure derivations. If a tab needs typing on, it's a design
  smell.
- Dropdowns (data validation) for Tier, Access path, Status, and Intro status — free text in
  those columns breaks the Dashboard counts.
- Portability: stick to COUNTA / COUNTIF / COUNTIFS / SUMIF / SUM / INDEX / MATCH / IF /
  IFERROR / TODAY so the same file works in Excel and Google Sheets. No FILTER/XLOOKUP unless
  the founder commits to one platform.
- Size the working range for ~50 investors (extend formulas, validation, and formatting
  together if the list outgrows it).

**Implementation traps (test-built in Excel and Google Sheets; these bite every time):**
- **Guard INDEX against blanks.** INDEX of an empty cell returns 0, which renders as `0` or the
  date 1899-12-30 in the view. Wrap every view pull:
  `=IFERROR(IF(INDEX(col,MATCH(k,helper,0))="","",INDEX(col,MATCH(k,helper,0))),"")`.
- **Don't count non-blanks with `"<>"`.** `COUNTIF(range,"<>")` and COUNTIFS `"<>"` behave
  differently across engines. Use COUNTA for non-blank counts, and for "warm rows with any intro
  status," enumerate the statuses in COUNTIFS (safe because the column is dropdown-locked).
- **Add an overflow check.** The view is capped at its row budget; a warm-intro investor past
  the cap silently disappears. Put a warning cell on the Dashboard comparing
  COUNTIF(access="Warm intro") against the view's populated rows.
- **Mark computed columns.** The helper column and all computed cells get grey styling and an
  "(auto)" label — overwriting the helper column silently empties the view, and sheet protection
  doesn't survive the Excel↔Google Sheets round-trip.

**Readability (a sheet the founder won't abandon):**
- Freeze the header row and the Partner column; bold white-on-dark headers; comfortable column
  widths; dates as `yyyy-mm-dd`.
- Conditional formatting that shows the state of the raise at a glance: status colors (greens
  for term sheet/committed, red for passed, amber for waiting-on-them — outreach sent, intro
  requested, no response), tier shading on A rows.
- A short legend on the Dashboard: which cells to edit (inputs), which are computed, what the
  statuses mean. Two or three clearly-fictional example rows **on the Pipeline tab** showing the
  expected format (at least one with a warm-intro path, so the Warm Intros view demonstrates
  itself).
- No formatting or validation sprayed over thousands of empty rows — keep it to the working
  range.

- **"Intro owner"** is the column that makes it work: every warm path has a named person
  responsible, and the founder chases owners, not funds.
- Statuses: researched → outreach sent / intro requested → meeting booked → meeting held →
  partner meeting → diligence → term sheet / committed / passed (+ note why).

## What to expect (set honest expectations)

| Benchmark | Figure | Source context |
|-----------|--------|----------------|
| Investors contacted (successful raise) | ~58 (seed) to ~71 (pre-seed) | DocSend/HBS surveys, 2015–2023 |
| Meetings | ~30–46 | same |
| Time to close | ~12–20 weeks | same; failed raises typically gave up at ~7 weeks |
| Meetings per term sheet | roughly 10:1 | practitioner benchmark, not a survey |
| Volume vs. outcome | contacting more investors does **not** correlate with raising more | DocSend |

Expect ~95% attrition from first contact to check — that's a normal raise, not a failing one.
Past ~100 qualified contacts with no close, the problem is usually the company or timing, not
the outreach — say that honestly.

## Principles

- **Fit beats volume.** Every hour spent on a non-fitting fund is wasted twice — theirs and the
  founder's.
- **Relationships before asks.** A premature intro request burns the connection; portfolio
  founders and advisors help when the relationship is real.
- **Never manufacture intimacy.** No fake familiarity, no exaggerating how well the founder knows
  the introducer (investors check), no implying traction that doesn't exist.
- **Never fabricate investor data.** Theses drift and funds die; verify against recent deals or
  mark `[VERIFY]`.
- **Say the hard thing.** If weak traction or poor thesis fit is the real blocker, better
  outreach won't fix it — name it, and suggest what would change the answer.

## What this skill does NOT do

- It does not send any email — it produces drafts and a plan for the founder to execute.
- It does not invent investor names, theses, portfolios, or relationships — unverified facts are
  marked `[VERIFY]` with a way to check them.
- It does not do mass automated outreach, and it does not recommend paid intro marketplaces or
  outsourced fundraisers — investors discount both.
- It does not prepare the materials investors will ask for next: run **`deck-review`** before
  outreach begins, **`dataroom-prep`** before diligence starts, and **`investor-update`** for the
  pre-raise relationship-building sends and for keeping backers (and friendly passes) warm after.
- It assumes **US pre-seed/seed venture** norms.
